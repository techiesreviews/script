# DEMO · How to Write a Good Video Script

- Channel: a made-up demo channel
- Status: demo script for script.techies.tools; every person and number in it is invented.
- Length: about 2.5 minutes spoken (about 400 words).
- Episode question: Why does my script read fine on paper and sound stiff on camera?
- Viewer takeaway: start with the problem, write for the ear, and read it out loud before you record.

How to read this: lines in normal text are spoken. `[ON SCREEN: …]` is for the edit. `[SILENT: …]` is a reminder for you, not to be read.

---

## 1 · Hook (0:00–0:20)

[ON SCREEN: a neat script on screen, then a presenter stumbling over the first line]

A blank page. A deadline. And a script that reads fine, until you say it out loud. Why does it sound so stiff on camera?

Most of us write a video like a blog post. Long sentences. Big words. Then we wonder why nobody stays past the first minute.

Hi, I'm Sam, and on this demo channel we make video a little less scary. Today: how to write a script that sounds like you, and how to know it works before you press record.

## 2 · Start with the problem (0:20–0:45)

[ON SCREEN: two first lines side by side: "In this video I will…" crossed out, "Your site went white this morning." ticked]

Your viewer doesn't care about your topic yet. They care about their problem. So your first line names it.

Not "In this video I will talk about backups." But: "Your site went white this morning. Now what?"

So that's the first rule. The problem first, then who you are, then what they get.

[SILENT: Keep this part short. No tools yet.]

## 3 · Write for the ear (0:45–1:10)

[ON SCREEN: one long sentence breaking into three short ones]

A script is not read. It's heard. And the ear can't scroll back.

So use short sentences, one idea each. Words you would use at the kitchen table, not in a report.

And when something is hard to picture, give it a picture. A backup is a spare key. A plugin is an add-on. Then show it on screen.

[ON SCREEN: three ticks: "short", "spoken words", "one picture"]

## 4 · Sound like you, not like AI (1:10–1:40)

[ON SCREEN: a script with three lines underlined: "Let's dive in", "In today's fast-paced world", "Here's the good news"]

AI helps you write faster. But it has favourite phrases, and viewers hear them right away. "Let's dive in." "In today's fast-paced world." "Here's the good news."

Mark those lines and say them the way you would to a friend. If you would never say it out loud, cut it.

[SILENT: Show the "Sounds like me" check in the editor here.]
[SILENT: Don't name a specific AI tool.]

## 5 · Read it out loud (1:40–2:05)

[ON SCREEN: someone reading their script out loud with a phone timer running]

Here's the part almost everyone skips. Before you record, read the whole script out loud. Once. With a timer.

Where do you stumble? That sentence is too long. Where do you get bored? That part can go.

If it sounds like you, you're ready. If not, you have a draft. Now you know, and today it costs you nothing.

## 6 · Make it a habit (2:05–2:20)

[ON SCREEN: a checklist: "problem first", "short sentences", "read out loud"]

Keep one checklist next to every script. Problem first. Short, spoken sentences. Read it out loud once.

That's five extra minutes per video. Compare that with a video nobody finishes.

## 7 · Recap and close (2:20–2:35)

[ON SCREEN: the three ticks again: "problem first", "for the ear", "read out loud"]

So, three things. Start with the problem. Write for the ear. And read it out loud before you record.

Tell me in the comments: which line do you always get stuck on? Mine is the first one. Every time.

Thanks for watching, and see you in the next one.

## Before recording

- Read the script out loud once, with a timer.
- Mark every line you stumbled on and make it shorter.
- Prepare the before/after first line for section 2.
